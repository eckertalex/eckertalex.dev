I don't have much of an AI setup. What I have is a preference for simple tools, applied to a much faster moving target than usual. Simple doesn't mean minimal or primitive to me. It means few moving parts, and parts I understand. The fewer assumptions I've built in, the less there is to go stale.

When a workflow starts feeling complicated, I get suspicious. Complexity is sometimes needed and some problems are genuinely hard, but most of the time when I notice something has gotten messy, it means I haven't found the best way to do it yet. AI makes this worse, or at least faster: workflows built around this week's model or this month's tool expire quickly. A simple piece is a lot more flexible, so it tends to survive the churn, and when something better comes along I can swap that one piece instead of tearing down a whole system built on last week's best practice.

I still stay informed and try things early. That's the only way to know whether something is useful or just hyped. But I look at everything I use with the same practical questions: what is this costing me, what happens when I stop using it, what am I getting for the complexity. Staying informed and staying invested are two different things, and mixing them up is how people end up stuck with tools for reasons that stopped applying a year ago. The other version of this is picking things up because everyone else is, without spending the time to understand it. Using a tool isn't the same as wielding it effectively.

My philosophy for AI is mostly the same as for any other tool: keep it simple, and don't use anything I haven't taken the time to actually understand.
